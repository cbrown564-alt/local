# The Tool Centre — elevation brief: two names, one counter

Written 4 August 2026, seventh in the ranked sequence in
`docs/elevation-brief-priorities.md`. The general method is in
`docs/the-elevation-method.md`; this is the Trade application of it,
derived from this shop's record — the ranking is explicit that the
register's big moves (the range mapped, the ride) belong to the
already-briefed Mourne Cycles, and this brief must find the counter's own
version. It is also a deliberately modest brief: the ranking put the shop
at seven because the recognition delta is editorial rather than
structural, and the record does not support manufactured spectacle. The
method's discipline for a thin inventory applies: make much of what
exists rather than add to it.

**Status: written 4 August 2026; not yet implemented.** The current
concept passes the publication checks and already stages the business's
real mechanism — the hire desk with its "day rate on request" board, the
firm's sourced counter number on every action, the two service lines
named, the seven-day hours carried from the directory. What it does not
have is a voice: the verification notes Google reviews praising the owner
and the prices, but no sentence was ever harvested, and not one word on
either route comes from the shop or its customers.

## The shape of this business

The Tool Centre is a **trade counter**: what the customer buys is access
— a tool for the day, gas for the heater, a straight answer from someone
who knows the stock — plus the expertise that comes free with it. The
theatre register is the counter's own, not the bike shop's: capability
for a day. You do not buy a stump grinder for one stump; you ring, you
ask, you collect it Saturday morning. The owner test: the person the
reviews praise opens the link on a phone, and what should land is a
customer's own sentence about the shop, the two names resolved into one
business, and the counter number doing the work. The concept competes
with a Facebook page behind Meta's login wall — and, uniquely in the
portfolio, with a search-results page: the same-named shops in
Newcastle-upon-Tyne dominate every query, so the shop's real web problem
is not an ugly site but invisibility in its own town.

## The essence

**Two service lines, two names, one counter — a shop that runs on being
asked, in a town that cannot find it to ask.**

The paradox is the gap between the trade and the trace: a seven-day
counter with directory-verified hours, a Calor dealership and customers
leaving praise for the owner and the prices — and an online presence
that is a login-walled Facebook page trading under a *different name*
from the directories, buried under an English city's results. Everything
the shop is, is local and verbal: ring the counter, ask the yard. The
concept's job is to give that verbal business a permanent address without
pretending it is a written one.

## What recognition can draw on

- **Their ritual:** the ask. Day rate on request; call the counter; the

- **Their arc:** none on record. No founding date, no turns, no names.

- **Their mark:** none held. The concept's black-and-yellow roundel is

## The diagnosis

1. **The page has no voice and does not admit it.** Every sentence on
   both routes is studio-written. The best material the record points at
   — customers praising the owner and the prices — sits unharvested in a
   verification note.
2. **The two-names tension is named but not structural.** "Hardware on
   the shelf. Hire on the yard." is the h1, and the Facebook trading
   style gets one bolded mention in the lede. The thing that makes this
   shop unlike any other — one counter answering for two trades under
   two names — organises nothing.
3. **The swap test passes on facts and fails on feel.** The sourced
   facts — address, phone, hours, the dual naming — are this shop alone.
   Strip them and the black-and-yellow hire board could front any tool
   shop in Ireland.
4. **The shop's real pain is in the case study, not the concept.** The
   Newcastle-upon-Tyne burial is the strongest argument the page has for
   existing, and the concept itself treats Co. Down placement as good
   practice rather than as the point.
5. **The rate story is told two ways.** The landing desk board asks
   callers to ring for rates; the hire list publishes indicative figures
   under a label. The publication record flags the case-study sources
   copy as needing consistency — a small, real honesty debt.

## The moves

Editorial weight throughout, per the ranking. Ordered by the recommended
build sequence: voice first, then the essence made visible; the theatre
here is the voice move doing double duty, and the keepable artifact is
gated on engagement.

### 1. Harvest the voice, then let a customer carry the page

The research action the verification has been waiting for: read the
Google reviews and harvest the best sentence — the ones the record says
praise the owner and the prices. One sentence, verbatim, attributed as a
Google review with its read date recorded, becomes the page's emotional
centre: the shop spoken for by someone who has paid at its counter. This
is the best-sentence test run on the only public voice the shop has, and
it doubles as the moment worth showing — the owner reading what a
customer bothered to write. The discipline is absolute: if the harvest
yields nothing clean, nothing ships — the page keeps its studio voice and
does not pretend otherwise.

### 2. One counter, two names

The essence made structural. Every screen resolves the two names into one
business — The Tool Centre, trading on Facebook as Tool Centre Plant
Hire, one address, one number — and the shelf/yard split becomes the
page's organising pair rather than a headline: hardware, gas and the
trade counter on one side; hire on the other; the counter bridging both.
The disambiguation moves from good practice to grammar: Newcastle,
Co. Down, under the Mournes, stated where a stranger would need it,
because the shop's actual web problem is being mistaken for a city three
hundred miles away.

### 3. Close the rate-sheet honesty loop

The publication-flagged repair, folded in: the landing desk board, the
hire list and the case-study sources copy tell one consistent story about
indicative rates — every figure labelled, every category understood as a
proposal, the "call for rates" position the same on all three surfaces.
The hire list's weekend multipliers and conditions are reviewed so
nothing reads as the shop's real terms. Small, and exactly the kind of
inconsistency an owner would catch first.

### 4. The real rate sheet, when the shop engages

The keepable artifact, gated: the hire list is one engagement away from
being the thing the counter prints — verified stock categories and real
rates replacing the illustrative lines, which the publication record
already names as the improvement path. Until then the sheet stays
labelled a proposal; afterwards it is the page a hire business actually
uses, and the one-sheet candidate. The brief's position: this gate is
also the pitch — the concept shows the counter what its own rate sheet
would look like with a permanent address.

## Recommended bundles

- **Research action first:** move 1's harvest, done before any build; the
  voice move ships only if a sentence earns it.
- **First pass (no new assets):** moves 2 and 3 — the two-names structure
  and the rate-story consistency — with move 1's quotation if the harvest
  succeeded.
- **On engagement:** move 4, and the one-sheet.

## Honesty constraints

- **Reviews are quoted verbatim, attributed and dated**, with the read
  date recorded in the research folder — the Kent pattern. No edited
  quotes, no star-rating claims (the record holds none), no review
  presented as the shop's own words. If a quoted review names the owner,
  that is the record gaining a name — source and date it; otherwise no
  names anywhere.
- **Every rate on every surface stays indicative and prominently
  labelled.** The landing board asks; the hire list proposes; the case
  study says so in the same words. No figure may read as the shop's
  current price.
- **No invented stock, services or terms.** The four hire categories and
  the Calor dealership are the verified list; no delivery, servicing,
  account or loyalty claims.
- **Hours as directory-listed only** — Mon–Sat 9–5, Sun 10–4, sourced to
  Big Red Directory, read 22 July 2026. Nothing more precise, nothing
  seasonal.
- **The arc stays empty.** No founding date, no history, no "serving
  Newcastle since" — the record holds none.
- **The mark stays concept work**, suggested by the shop's badge and
  never presented as the badge.
- **The disambiguation is permanent:** Co. Down on every route; nothing
  from the same-named English businesses may touch the page.
- Any change to the first viewport recaptures the comparison stills and
  clip in the same commit, per `docs/MEDIA_CAPTURE.md`.

## Tests that pin the elevation

- If the voice move ships: assert the quotation is verbatim against the
  harvested source note, carries its attribution and date, and that no
  star rating or unquoted review content appears anywhere.
- Assert no owner name appears on either route unless it arrives inside a
  sourced quotation recorded in research.
- A standing scan asserts every £ figure on both routes sits under the
  indicative label, and the desk board contains no figures at all.
- Assert the naming resolution and "Co. Down" appear on both routes, and
  the desk board, hire list and case-study sources copy use the same
  indicative-rates wording.
- Run the guest-voice guard and the publication checks; then the swap
  test: remove the address, the phone, the hours and the two names — if
  the page still works for any tool shop, the elevation has not gone far
  enough.

## Open decisions

- Are Google reviews quotable for this use — verbatim, attributed, dated
  — under the same pattern as Kent's BBC memories, or does third-party
  review content wait for the shop's own engagement? This brief's
  position: a public review, quoted plainly as one, is honest; but the
  harvest should be read by a second pair of eyes before it ships.
- Does the two-names restructure change the h1 the case-study media
  already shows? If so, the recapture cost is real and the move should be
  weighed against it.
- If the harvest fails, does the page ship moves 2–3 voiceless, or wait?
  The structure moves stand alone; the brief recommends shipping them and
  leaving the voice slot designed but empty.
- Does the illustrative hire list lead the outreach, or does outreach
  lead with the landing page and hold the sheet back as the engagement
  ask? The gate works both ways; the pitch order is the project owner's
  call.

## When a move ships

Record the source and read date for every new fact — the harvested review
above all — add provenance for any new asset in the same commit, update
the case-study Sources & limits block, and pin new behaviour in the
verification tooling. Any move that changes the first viewport recaptures
the comparison stills and clip in the same commit. Update this status
line as moves land; do not call the concept validated until an owner or
representative visitor has supplied that evidence.
