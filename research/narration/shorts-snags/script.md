# Shorts I — Five snags

*10 August 2026. Piece 4 of five in
[`../../narrative-audio-plan.md`](../../narrative-audio-plan.md) §4.
Voice: Old Irish Village Elder (`eEzkfaTvgdaH5to7Cn0M`). Model `eleven_v3`.
Five scripts, ~4,070 characters total, ~50–60s each.*

**Source.** Every beat comes from `src/site/data/fault-walks.ts` — the same
five entries the `/where-it-fails/` page walks. Each script uses four fields
from its entry: `errand` (what the visitor came to do), the walk itself,
`decision` (why the owner chose it), and `check` (the takeaway question).

**The `decision` field is doing the most work here.** Without it these are five
accusations in a row, which is unlistenable and also unfair — the page already
knows the owner's choice was reasonable at the time. Each script therefore
turns once: *here is what it costs* → *and here is why it was a sensible thing
to have done*. The check lands last, as a question, never a verdict.

**Voice rules.** No place names. No business named. Guest vocabulary per
[`../../owner-voice-on-the-three-pages.md`](../../owner-voice-on-the-three-pages.md)
— *the snag*, not *fault*. Second person throughout: these address the owner
directly, which the long pieces do not.

**Honesty.** No prevalence claims. Nothing asserts how often any of this
happens or what fixing it would produce. The visitor in each script is
explicitly a made-up visitor doing a named errand, exactly as the page's
invented mocks are.

## S1 — The login at the door

Someone wants to see what you sell. That's all.

They find your page. And before anything else, a box asks them to accept
cookies. Behind it, greyed out, is your name.

They accept. Now it wants them to log in. Or create an account.

[pause]

They wanted to look in the window. They've been asked for a password.

Now — nobody chose this. The page was free, it took ten minutes, and the wall
came with it. It felt like having a website. That's a reasonable thing to have
thought.

But here's what it costs. A stranger will not make an account to find out
whether you're worth visiting. They'll go and look at somebody who lets them
look.

So the check is one question. Can a visitor see what you sell — without an
account?

If not, that's the first thing to fix. Before the colours. Before anything.

## S2 — Nobody answers "are you open?"

Someone's on their way into the town. They want to know one thing. Are you open
right now.

They land on your site. There's a bar across the top offering to translate the
page. Underneath it, a video plays.

They scroll. There's a map. They scroll again.

[pause]

Two screens down, there it is. Tuesday to Sunday, nine to four.

So now they have to work out what day it is, what time it is, and do the sum
themselves. Standing outside. In the rain.

The hours were on the site. They were always on the site — that's the
frustrating part. This isn't missing information. It's buried information. The
translate bar was recommended. The video was paid for. Both felt like
improvements.

The fix is one line, at the top. Open now — until four today.

So: does your first screen say whether you're open?

## S3 — The bell with no clapper

Someone wants to ask if the fourteenth is free.

They find the page. There's a button that says Book. They tap it.

It gives them a phone number.

[pause]

Now, a phone number isn't nothing. But it's half nine at night, which is when
people plan things. They're not going to ring. They'll ring tomorrow — except
tomorrow they're at work, and then it's the weekend, and by then they've booked
somewhere else.

"Get in touch" means the visitor does the work.

And the reason it's like that is honest enough. A form felt like a big build.
The number was already on the page. Everybody knows how a phone works.

But the asking can happen on the page itself. The date. The number of people. A
button that says Ask.

So: can someone ask about a date, on the page, at half nine at night?

## S4 — The best thing about you is in the footer

Someone's choosing between you and two others. They're looking for a reason.

They land on your page. It says what you do. It says it the way everyone who
does that says it.

They scroll past the services. They scroll past the photographs.

And down at the very bottom, in small grey text — the years. The award. The
accreditations.

[pause]

The thing you'd say in ten seconds at the counter isn't on the site at all.
It's in the footer, where modest things go to hide.

And it went there for a good reason. Saying it out loud felt like boasting.

But a visitor isn't a neighbour who already knows you. They came with one
question — why you — and it got answered last, in the smallest text on the
page.

So: is the best reason to choose you the first thing on the page?

---

## S5 — The swap test

This one's quick. And it's the one people don't like.

Open your website. Look at the top of the page. The picture, the heading, the
first sentence.

Now imagine swapping your name for a competitor's.

[pause]

Does anything else need to change?

If it doesn't — the site isn't yours. It's a template with your name in the
corner.

And this is the least stupid mistake on the list. A clean template with the
name dropped in looks safe. Professional. It looks like a proper business.

It also looks like everyone else's.

The way out isn't a fancier design. It's the opposite. It's the specific
things. What you actually sell. How you actually work. The thing that would be
a lie if a competitor said it.

So: swap your name. Would anything else need to change?
